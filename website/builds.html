5 fun ways to get started

Pick one, paste the prompt into an AI app and fill in the [brackets]

What's for dinner?

5 min / Any app with photos

Three dinners you can make tonight, plus a tiny shopping list

Here's a photo of my fridge. Give me three dinners I can make tonight with what's in here, plus anything I'd need to grab. Keep each one under 30 minutes

Teach it your voice

15 min / Custom instructions / ▶︎ ep 005

A one-page voice guide you can reuse everywhere

Interview me one question at a time about how I write: who I usually write to, words I use a lot, and things I'd never say. After eight questions, write a short voice guide I can paste into my custom instructions

What we made Mary's guide was built from her real emails and texts. It knows she says "thank you so big," signs off "Fired up," and never uses a long dash

Your own coach

15 min / Projects / ▶︎ ep 004

A plan for this week and a coach that remembers last week

You're my coach for [goal]. Start by asking me five questions about where I am now and what gets in my way. Then give me a plan for this week. Every time I come back, ask how it went before you give advice. Be honest, not a cheerleader

What we made We built a coach and a logistics helper for our El Cruce trail race, then had to coach the coach. It's all in ep 004

Build a tiny app

10 min / Vibe coding

A working bill splitter you can open on your phone

Make me a simple, colorful web app that splits a dinner bill. I type the total, the tip and everyone's names, and it shows what each person owes. Make it easy to use on my phone

What we made This whole website. We described it and Claude built it

Settle a debate

10 min / Thinking partner

The strongest version of each side, and what would change each mind

My partner and I disagree about [topic]. Give the strongest case for each side, then tell us what evidence would change each of our minds. Don't pick a winner

The ten-hour rule Wharton professor Ethan Mollick's rule of thumb: AI starts to click after about ten hours of real use. Use it on stuff you already know, so you can tell when it's wrong

Set up the tracker

Attach the tracker first

We're planning our wedding and I've attached a starter tracker. Before you change anything, interview us one question at a time: the date and place, a rough guest count and budget, which events we're hosting (a welcome party, a rehearsal dinner, a breakfast the day after), and what we care about and what we'll skip. Ask about anything first-timers usually forget. Then rewrite the tracker for us: cut what we're not doing, add what's missing, and give every task a category, a due date, an owner, a status and a priority

Compare venue packages

I've attached the full wedding packages from [venues]. Make a side by side table of what each would really cost for [guest count] guests on [date]: the base rate, required add-ons, minimums, fees and what's not included. Flag anything buried in the fine print, like a required planner. Then tell me what I could ask each venue to flex on

Plan a venue tour

We're touring [venue] for our wedding and neither of us has planned one before. Give us the questions first-timers forget to ask, grouped by cost, food and drinks, and the day itself. Star the ones that could change the price

Preview the decor

Here's a photo of our venue empty, one of it set up for someone else's wedding, and our flower plan. Show our tables with our flowers instead of theirs. Then make three versions with [color 1], [color 2] and [color 3] linens so we can compare them side by side

Sort your vows brain dump

Here's a voice memo of me rambling about [partner] for way too long. Don't write my vows. Pull out every memory, every quality I admire and every funny moment I mentioned, in my own words, grouped. Then ask me which ones matter most
